## Part B - Survey Evaluation:

Identify and describe survey features:

```
1. Sample type: Stratified simple random sample without replacement within each stratum (= geographic areas)

2. Sample size: The target sample size was 20,000, while the actual number of respondents was 16,149.

3. Target population: The target population for the survey included all persons 15 years of age and older in Canada, excluding:
Residents of the Yukon, Northwest Territories, and Nunavut, and Full-time residents of institutions.

4. Sampling frame: created using two different components:
- Lists of telephone numbers in use (both landline and cellular) available to Statistics Canada from various sources (Telephone companies, Census of population, etc.);
- The Address Register (AR): List of all dwellings within the ten provinces.

5. Survey mode(s): invitation letters. Data were collected electronically as a self-completed questionnaire (rEQ) as well as via computer assisted telephone interviews (iEQ).

6. Timeline: Data collected from September 4th to December 28th, 2018. Reference period: Past 12 months preceding interview date.

7. Response rate: The overall response rate was 41.9%.

8. Weights: The number of persons represented by a given person in the sample is usually known as the weight or weighting factor of the sampled person.
Weight calculations depended on: 
- Initial household weight 
- Three-stage non-response adjustment
- Person weight calculation (household weight × Factor 1 × number of eligible household members)
- Adjustment for rejective sampling
- Adjustments to external totals (stratum, income, province-age-sex, raking ratio adjustments)
∑ WGHT_PERi = 30,843,019 = Estimate of the number of persons aged 15 and over in the population.

9. Data processing:
- Data capture: same data capture system for rEQ and iEQ, through computers.
- Coding: For write-in responses: coded into existing categories (where a match was possible), grouped into new categories or left in “other-specify” (if a match with an existing category was not possible or frequencies were too small to create a new category). When possible, coded using standard classification systems.
- Edit and imputation: verification was performed to identify and eliminate potential duplicate records and to drop non-response and out-of-scope records. Clean-up: records that were determined to be out-of-scope and to be nonresponse were removed from the data file. Flow editing using 'top down' strategy. Donor imputation used for missing values.
- Creation of combined and derived variables to facilitate data analysis.
- Additional public use microdata file processing in order to preserve confidentiality.

10. Cleaning, imputation, etc:
Imputations were made using donor records selected through a score function. Characteristics on each record with item or partial non response (also called a recipient record) were compared with the characteristics on all donor records. When a characteristic was the same on the donor record and the recipient record, the donor's score increased. The donor record with the highest score was deemed the "nearest" donor and was chosen to fill in the missing information of the non respondent.
9 different steps:
- First: imputing personal income and family income. 
- The next three steps involved imputing the formal volunteering variables in the master file. 
- Steps five and six were imputing the informal volunteering variables in the master file. 
- The last three steps involved imputing variables in the donation file and the solicitation methods in the master file.

11. Sources of error:
a) Non-sampling errors
- For each phase of the survey operation. Example: Interviewers may misunderstand instructions, respondents may make errors in answering questions, the answers may be incorrectly entered into the EQ system, and errors may be introduced in the processing and tabulation of the data.
- Dffect of non-response on survey results. Example: Failure to answer one or more questions, interviewer was unable to contact the respondent, no household member was able to provide the information, or the respondent refused to participate in the survey...
b) Sampling errors
The chances are about 68 out of 100 that the difference between a sample estimate and the true population value would be less than one standard error, about 95 out of 100 that the difference would be less than two standard errors, and virtually certain that the differences would be less than three standard errors.

12. Limitations, known biases, etc:
- It was the first time survey was conducted online: tt is impossible to determine with certainty whether, and to what extent, differences in a variable are attributable to an actual change in the population or to changes in the survey methodology. 
- population not covered: household without telephones.
- population less represented: young males (15-24 years old) known to respond less will be under-represented
```
